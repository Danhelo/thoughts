## Thoughts:
1. First session: I recall reading the first 20 pages on Siddartha's discontent with what the knew about the world. An inquisitive mind, he set out to be a Samana, an ascetic monk in the forests of India. His friend and shadow Govinda followed him. 
	* After years of denying the Self through voluntary suffering, he realized this is just another form of 'coping' with the Self, like alcohol or sex. I related it to Catholic and Western spirituality. Still denying the Self in this way raises our baseline dopamine so to speak, enabling Siddartha to be more present and appreciative of the world (later realized after his talk with Buddha).
	* Siddartha is the reader. The inquisitive mind seeking wisdom from a book called Siddartha. While the language is cultural and specific, we can all relate to his struggles of identity and desire.
	* Siddartha comes to the realization that all teaching is futile. It will always be hijacked by the Self. As such, there is no learning, no knowledge, he only understands this with the help of Buddha. Be wary of my own mind, since it may hide ignorance with cleverness
1. Second Session: The next 20 pages have Siddartha talk to the Buddha and start anew. I can't believe it this was crazy.
	* Sid realized that no Teaching would fulfill him, even the Buddha's apparently perfect doctrine. After pointing out a flaw to the Enlightened One, he responded to Sid by asking him if he did not belive in self realization at all. Sid, however, replied cleverly, pointing out that any one path obfuscates the ture innermost core.
	* The Buddha smiled at him, recognizing the awareness, but warned him of too much cleverness. *Be careful of too much cleverness*. 
	* Sid was **awakened**, realizing that his identity was tied up with the pursuit of knowledge. Knowledge and thoughts are just as phenomenological as the sensory world, and both form the true essence of reality. Truth is found within things, not past them. 
	* With this realization, Sid embraces himself and starts pursuing himself after any idea. This leads him back to society, towards Kamala and his innermost core speaks to him a goal to have her teach him love.
	* Siddartha pursues the goal seemingly incredibly well and efficiently, shocking the now loving Kamala. This shock is no mystery to meditated folks. It is clear that, like a stone dropping in water, Siddartha attracts his goals without any action. All apparent effort, hesitation, and challenge (even mental) is part of the phenomenological world he has overcome through **contemplation, patience, and templance**. Or in his words, by knowing how to **think, wait, and fast**. These really are all the skills I need in life. The rest is my mind confusing my true self, not the apparent one it constructs. My innermost core, the Holy Spirit in Christianity, always has and always will know what to do. 
	
	Rest of the Sessions:
	I finished the book couldn't contain myself. Be wary of thoughts and knowledge but here I go:
	Siddartha pursues Samsara, the game of the world, driven to find Kamala's love. Because of his virtues, he finds the game easy to play and quickly becomes rich and successful. His disdain and judgement of the child-people gets tested as he sees more and more of them, mentored but eventually surpassing Kamaswami. He is wise, and treats beggar and king alike. However, as the years fly by, the wheel of his innermost core starts spinning slower and slower. His Samananess, while always in him, starts being suffocated.
	
	Siddartha loses his way in Sansara. The world very slowly captures him. He becomes a gambling addict in a twisted love for destroying the wealth he nonetheless seeks. Depression sets in and one day, meditating under his Mango tree, he knows something has to change.
	Siddartha leaves Kamala behind, bearing a child. He longed death, and attempted suicide by a river. As he let himself sink deeper and deeper, a voice in him resounded.
	
	He recognized the foolishness of his actions in an act of rebirth. He was able to recognize and truly disdain the things of the world, no longer only in knowledge (as when he was young), but subjectively with body and soul. That old rich Siddartha died in the river.
	
	Siddartha slept and slept, meeting Govinda by the river. A true wanderer, he runs accross the Ferryman he met years ago, when he was setting out for the city.
	The Ferryman initially dismisses him for his rich clothing, but soon realizes it is Siddartha, having grown past his material life. Siddartha expresses his admiration for the Ferryman, how he can find peace by the river. They quickly become friends and the River becomes master of both.
	 
	 Siddartha learns to listen to the river. He wants to become as good a listener as the Ferryman. However, he soon learns that listening is all he has to do. They live a simple life together, learning from the river. Siddartha recognizes that time is an illusion in the River's shape, and starts hearing the thousands of voices that emanate from it. They all chant "Om". But he is still seeking enlightenment. 
	 
	One day, on the day the Buddha was set to pass away, Kamala and her son go out to greet the Perfect One alongside a lot of other followers of Gautama. They reach the river, an impasse to many. Kamala is bitten by a snake and passes away in the bed that the Ferryman's wife died. Siddartha sees his life with her as she dies, both the young and old versions of eachother. As Kamala recognizes him, she sees the Buddha, he who she was seeking. Siddartha reassures her of her peace in death.
	
	Siddartha is left with his son. He forms an unexplainable attachment to him. A spoiled and tough-hearted kid, his son becomes a great source of suffering for the group. Siddartha in his infinite patience and kindness becomes a torturer, an incredibly boring subjucator to his son. Still, he can't set him free, for he has become a child-person, driven by the passions of fatherhood and the follies of love. He even becomes jealous of passerbys with sons, especially after the one he has leaves and escapes. That night he followed him into the city, the grove where he met Kamala. There he realized he had to endure this wound of sonlessness, as the Ferryman brought him back.

	The wound endured for a long time, helping Siddartha feel more empathetic towards the child-people. He respected their passions and could love them for it. Slowly, he realized what wisdom truly was. It was listening, readiness of the soul for the embrace of oneness, of what is as it truly is. What everything is and always has been. 
	
	One day Siddartha set out to look for his son. As he left, he heard the river laughing at him. In his reflection he saw his father, and listening to the river, saw himself suffering just as his father had when he left him. An endless cycle of the same actions over and over, a comedy. Now he understood the river's laughs. But still he suffered from his wound.

	He opened himself up entirely to Vasudeva. He was the best listener in the world. Together, they listened to the river, and heard the voice of constantly flowing lament in it. Vasudeva pressured Siddartha to listen better.

	He heard the neverending voices of longing, suffering longing, of Kamala, of Govinda, his fater and his son, flowing into one another. The river was made of all the people he had ever seen, waves rushing against one another, water becoming steam, sky, rainfal, and stream. The voice changed. It sounded searchingly, but other voices joined it, joy and sorrow, good and evil, a thousand voices.

	Siddartha was now a sponge, an all listener. He was no longer able to distinguish the voices, they were he river of occurrences, the music of life. And when he litened neither for sorrow or laughter, without binding his Self to what he heard, when he heard the entirety, the oneness, it all sang Om: Perfection. His wound blossomed and his Self flowed into Oneness.

	 Thus Siddartha stopped struggling with fate, with suffering, belonging to Oneness. Vasudeva, the Enlightened, left for the forest.

	Govinda went to talk to the wise Ferryman others referred to. He went and asked how to achieve Enlightenement. Siddartha told him that he perhaps seeks too much, that seeking does not allow him to find, since it narrows the eye to what he seeks. Finding, is being open to the Oneness. 

	After recognizing Siddartha, Govinda shyly remarked he had become a ferryman. Siddartha laughed warmly, noting how some people must make themselves anew many times. 

	The following morning, Govinda implored Siddartha to share with him knowledge. Siddartha explained that he has no Teaching, that he still distrusts teachers, as he believes wisdom cannot be taught, unlike knowledge, it must be found and then lived. He explained still, that he had many teachers, the ascetics, Kamala, Govinda by the river, but most of all, the River and the Ferryman. 

	Siddartha's insights for the seekers are:
	* Wisdom cannot be conveyed, only found, lived, and carried by it. 
	* For every truth, the opposite is equally true. In fact, truth can only be spoken and shrouded in words when it is one sided. Everything thought is one sided, lacking Oneness. Sansara and Nirvana, illusion and truth. The world, however, Being, is never onesided. Never is a person entirely Nirvana or Sansara. It seems like so because we are subject to time, but time is not real. Separation is only imaginary. 
	* The sinner will one day be Buddha, but the one day is an illusion. We must worship the future, the potential, the Buddha in the sinner, since it is already entirely within him. The sinner is not on a path to Buddhahood, but our brains may not imagine things otherwise. 
	* The world is complete at every moment, every sin carrying grace, all present carrying past and future: infants are already dead and the dead are already in eternal life. 
	* In deep meditation there is the possiblity of suspending time, to see all life that has been, is being, and will be. 
	* Everything is Brahman, Oneness, Heaven, Holy, True Substrate, that is why everthing that *is* seems good to the Enlightened One. In fact, everything only requires our loving consent to be experienced. Siddartha needed the most shameful despair from sin, needed sin, to give up resisting to this love, to no longer compare the world to the one he longed for. 
	* One can truly love the world and learn from it like the river, or like a bee or cloud, but teachings have no world, only words. Things can teach, not words, even if things are illusory, since we would also be semblance, their equals. For this we can love things as they are.
	* Also for this are actions more valuable than teachings. Gautama clearly loved the world, even if he taught that love was a forbiddable shackle. (not true love lol).
	* True enlightenment is acceptance of Oneness as it is. Of everything. True listening. 

	Govinda was in conflict with these thoughts, and as he grappled with the conflict in his heart, from so many teachings, he kissed Siddartha in his forehead. As he still attempted in vain and with reluctance to think away time, to imagine Nirvana and Sansara as one, time faded away. He no longer saw Siddartha's face, but a thousand faces, al of them there at once and changing into one another in an instant, he saw death, suffering, murder, joy, birth, raging love, sex, gods, Kirshna, bulls, birds, each helping the other, loving them, hating them, destroying and giving birth to them anew. Each was a wish to die, a painful confession of transience, and yet none died only transfermed itself. And all these shapes flowed and rested swam away, and above all of them, was something thin, unsubstantial, a veil of Being, a mask that was also on Siddartha's smiling face. This smile of Oneness was the thousandfold, perhaps mocking, perhaps kind, smile of Gautama, the Buddha. This is how Perfect Ones smile.

	 As Govinda stood away from Siddartha, with a smile very similar to the Perfect One's, he no longer knew wheter Siddartha, or Gautama, or I myself existed, injured by a Divine arrow. Govinda bowed deeply and the feeling of the most intense love and humble reverence overcame him before the one who sat motionless. The one whose smile reminded him of everything in his ife that he had ever loved, that was ever dear and holy to him. Govinda was Enlightened.
